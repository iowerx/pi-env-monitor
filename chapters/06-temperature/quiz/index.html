    3. 6. Temperature - From the Thermoscope to the Silicon Chip

    Quiz: Temperature: From the Thermoscope to the Silicon Chip¶

    Test your understanding of what temperature is, heat transfer, the history of thermometers, the three temperature scales, electrical thermometers, and the Stevenson screen with these review questions.

    Value or Difference?

    Before you convert any temperature, ask whether it is a single reading or a change between two readings. That one check stops the most common conversion mistake.

    1. What does temperature actually measure?¶

    1. The total amount of heat energy stored in an object
    2. The amount of an invisible fluid called caloric in an object
    3. How fast energy flows from one object into another
    4. The average kinetic energy of the atoms or molecules in a substance
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of atoms, which is how fast they are moving. Hot means fast, and cold means slow. Option A confuses temperature with total energy. A swimming pool and a cup of water at the same temperature have the same average motion, but the pool holds far more energy. Option B is the old caloric theory, which Count Rumford disproved. Option C describes heat transfer.

    Concept Tested: Temperature and Kinetic Energy Of Atoms

    See: What Is Actually Happening When Something Is Hot

    2. In 1654, Ferdinand II of Tuscany sealed the liquid inside the glass. Which problem with the open thermoscope did this fix?¶

    1. Its readings also changed when the air pressure changed
    2. It had no marks, so it could not give a number
    3. Thermometers from different makers did not agree
    4. It took too long to respond to temperature changes
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Galileo's thermoscope had a stem open to the air, so its water level also moved when atmospheric pressure changed. Sealing the liquid created the liquid in glass thermometer and removed that error in one step. Option B was fixed earlier, when Santorio added marks around 1612. Option C stayed a problem until Fahrenheit's scale in 1724. Option D is not a problem the chapter describes.

    Concept Tested: Thermoscope and Liquid In Glass Thermometer

    See: Getting a Scale

    3. Where does the Kelvin scale put its zero?¶

    1. At the freezing point of pure water
    2. At the temperature of an ice, water, and salt mixture
    3. At absolute zero, where atomic motion reaches its minimum
    4. At the coldest temperature ever recorded on Earth
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, −273.15 °C, the point where atomic motion reaches its minimum. Nothing can be colder. Because its zero means "none," kelvin is a ratio scale, and 200 K really is twice 100 K. Option A is the zero of the Celsius scale. Option B is the zero of the Fahrenheit scale. Option D is not a physical boundary, since colder temperatures exist elsewhere.

    Concept Tested: Kelvin Scale and Absolute Zero

    See: Three Scales

    4. In 1800, William Herschel placed a control thermometer just past the red end of a spectrum of sunlight. It read warmer than the thermometers in the colored bands. What did this reveal?¶

    1. Red light carries more energy than any other visible color
    2. The prism itself was heating up and warming the thermometer
    3. Thermometers read higher when they are in the dark
    4. Invisible radiation beyond red light also carries energy
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Herschel had found infrared radiation, which has wavelengths longer than visible red light. No eye could see it, but it carried energy that warmed his thermometer. Option A is wrong because the thermometer was past the red band, not in it. Options B and C do not explain why only that spot was warmer. Today infrared is the basis of thermal cameras, non-contact thermometers, and the greenhouse effect.

    Concept Tested: Infrared Radiation

    See: Radiation You Cannot See

    5. You open a freezer door and feel cool air on your face. What is actually happening, according to the chapter?¶

    1. Heat is flowing from the warmer room into the colder freezer
    2. Cold is flowing out of the freezer into the warmer room
    3. Cold and heat are trading places at the same rate
    4. The freezer is giving off infrared rays that feel cold
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Heat transfer is the movement of thermal energy from a warmer object to a cooler one, and on its own it always runs that way. Cold is not a substance. It is just less atomic motion. So when you open a freezer, cold does not flow out (B). Heat flows in. Option C treats cold as a real thing that moves. Option D is wrong because objects emit infrared as heat, not as "cold."

    Concept Tested: Heat Transfer

    See: What Is Actually Happening When Something Is Hot

    6. On a clear summer afternoon, the station reports 30 °C while the asphalt nearby is 60 °C. What best explains the difference?¶

    1. The asphalt thermometer must be broken, because the two should match
    2. Air always warms up faster than solid ground does in sunlight
    3. The station's shaded thermometer reads lower than the real air temperature
    4. Air temperature and surface temperature are different quantities
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Air temperature is the temperature of the air, measured in shade with good airflow. Surface temperature is the temperature of a solid surface like asphalt. Sunlit asphalt can reach 60 °C when the air is 30 °C. Neither number is wrong (A). Option B gets it backward here, since the asphalt is far hotter. Option C is a misconception, because shade is part of how air temperature is correctly measured.

    Concept Tested: Air Temperature and Surface Temperature

    See: Radiation You Cannot See

    7. Your station reads 25 °C. What is that temperature in Fahrenheit?¶

    1. 45 °F
    2. 77 °F
    3. 57 °F
    4. 298 °F
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Temperature conversion from Celsius to Fahrenheit needs both a ratio and an offset: 25 × 9/5 + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77 °F. Option A applies the ratio but forgets the offset, which is only correct for a temperature change. Option C adds 32 but skips the ratio. Option D mixes in the Kelvin offset of 273.15. A quick check: doubling 25 and adding 30 gives 80, which is close to 77.

    Concept Tested: Temperature Conversion

    See: Converting Between Scales

    8. You check a lab thermometer marked in kelvins. You place it in ice water, then in boiling water, both at standard pressure. Which pair of readings shows it is correct?¶

    1. 0 K and 100 K
    2. 273.15 K and 373.15 K
    3. 32 K and 212 K
    4. −273.15 K and −173.15 K
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at standard pressure. To convert to kelvins, add 273.15, which gives 273.15 K and 373.15 K. Option A uses the Celsius numbers with the wrong unit. Option C uses the Fahrenheit freezing and boiling points. Option D subtracts instead of adding, and kelvin values can never be negative, because 0 K is absolute zero.

    Concept Tested: Freezing Point Of Water and Boiling Point Of Water

    See: Three Scales

    9. A pottery club wants to log the temperature inside a kiln that reaches 1200 °C. Which sensor fits this job?¶

    1. A thermistor
    2. A BME280 silicon diode
    3. A thermocouple
    4. A platinum resistance thermometer
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A thermocouple uses the thermoelectric effect: two different metals joined together produce a voltage that depends on temperature. Some types work from −200 °C to over 1700 °C, which is why they are used in furnaces and kilns. A thermistor (A) works only to about 150 °C. The BME280 (B) stops at 85 °C. A resistance thermometer (D) reaches about 850 °C, which is still too low.

    Concept Tested: Thermocouple and Thermoelectric Effect

    See: The Thermocouple

    10. Station A's sensor hangs on a sunny brick wall. Station B's sensor sits in a Stevenson screen 1.5 m above grass. In the afternoon, Station A reads 4 °C warmer. Which data should you trust for air temperature, and why?¶

    1. Station B, because the screen gives shade, airflow, and a standard height
    2. Station A, because sunshine is part of the real outdoor temperature
    3. Neither station, because one of the two sensors must be broken
    4. An average of both, because the true air temperature lies between them
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A Stevenson screen shields instruments from direct sunlight while letting air flow freely, at a standard height. Station A is absorbing sunlight and heat from the wall, so it measures itself and the wall, not the air. Option B misunderstands what air temperature means. Option C is wrong because both sensors can be working correctly. Averaging (D) just mixes good data with biased data.

    Concept Tested: Stevenson Screen

    See: Where You Put It Matters as Much as What You Buy
