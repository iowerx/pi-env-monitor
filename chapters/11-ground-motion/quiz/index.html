    3. 11. Ground Motion - Measuring Earthquakes

    Quiz: Ground Motion: Measuring Earthquakes¶

    Test your understanding of faults, seismic waves, seismometers, magnitude and intensity scales, MEMS accelerometers, and earthquake early warning with these review questions.

    Event or Place?

    Before answering a question about earthquake size, ask whether it describes the earthquake itself or the shaking at one place. That one check sorts out most magnitude and intensity questions.

    1. What could Zhang Heng's seismoscope of 138 AD do?¶

    1. Draw a continuous record of how long the shaking lasted
    2. Measure the magnitude of each earthquake it detected
    3. Predict earthquakes several days before they happened
    4. Detect that ground motion occurred and point toward its direction
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. A seismoscope detects and shows that ground motion has happened, without making a continuous record. Zhang Heng's bronze vessel dropped a ball from one of eight dragons into a toad's mouth, showing the direction the shaking came from. It could not record strength or length of shaking (A), so it could not measure magnitude (B). Option C is wrong, because nobody can predict earthquakes.

    Concept Tested: Seismoscope

    See: The Oldest Instrument in This Book

    2. What is an earthquake, according to the chapter?¶

    1. The sudden release of stored elastic energy when a fault slips
    2. The slow, steady sliding of plates at a few centimetres a year
    3. A wave of hot liquid rock rising from the Earth's outer core
    4. The bending of rock that happens while plates are locked together
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A fault is a fracture in the crust where rock masses move relative to each other. Friction locks the plates, and the rock bends and stores elastic energy (D), like a stick before it snaps. An earthquake is the sudden release of that energy when the fault slips. Option B describes normal plate motion, which causes no shaking on its own. Option C is not how the chapter describes earthquakes.

    Concept Tested: Earthquake and Fault

    See: Faults and Earthquakes

    3. Which seismic wave arrives first and can travel through liquid?¶

    1. The S wave, which is a shear wave
    2. The P wave, which is a compressional wave
    3. The S wave, which is a compressional wave
    4. The P wave, which is a shear wave
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The P wave is a compressional wave that pushes and pulls rock along its direction of travel, like sound. It moves about 6 km/s, arrives first, and can pass through liquid. The S wave is a shear wave that moves rock side to side. It is slower, does most of the damage, and cannot cross liquid. Options C and D swap the wave types. Option A names the slower wave.

    Concept Tested: P Wave and S Wave

    See: Seismic Waves

    4. How does a seismometer measure shaking when everything around it is shaking too?¶

    1. It compares the shaking to a second seismometer far away from the earthquake
    2. It measures how much the bolted-down frame moves compared with the air
    3. A suspended mass tends to stay still while the frame moves with the ground
    4. A heavy mass is bolted firmly to the ground so that it shakes the most
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Inertial mass is the property of matter that resists changes in motion. A seismometer hangs a heavy mass from a spring or wire on a frame bolted to the ground. When the ground shakes, the frame moves, but the mass tends to stay put. The relative motion between them is the ground motion. Option D gets it backward, because a bolted mass would move with everything else. Options A and B are not the method.

    Concept Tested: Inertial Mass and Seismometer

    See: Measuring Against Something That Will Not Move

    5. A news report says an earthquake was "magnitude 6.2" and caused "intensity VII" in the town of Riverton. Which statement is correct?¶

    1. Both numbers describe the shaking felt in Riverton
    2. The 6.2 describes the earthquake itself, and VII describes the shaking in Riverton
    3. The VII describes the energy released, and 6.2 describes the shaking in Riverton
    4. Both numbers describe the earthquake and would be the same in every town
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Magnitude describes the earthquake itself, and it has one value everywhere. Intensity describes the shaking at one place. The Mercalli intensity scale rates it with Roman numerals from I to XII, based on observed effects. Option C reverses the two. Options A and D each treat both numbers as the same kind of measure. Nearby towns would report different intensities for the same 6.2 earthquake.

    Concept Tested: Magnitude Versus Intensity and Mercalli Intensity Scale

    See: Magnitude Versus Intensity

    6. Why do seismologists use the moment magnitude scale instead of the Richter scale for very large earthquakes?¶

    1. Moment magnitude is based on how many people felt the shaking
    2. Moment magnitude is easier to calculate from a single seismogram
    3. The Richter scale saturates, while moment magnitude measures the rupture itself
    4. The Richter scale is not logarithmic, so it cannot show large differences
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Above about magnitude 7, the Richter scale saturates. Its readings stop growing with the true size of the event. The moment magnitude scale uses the physical rupture: rock rigidity, fault area, and slip distance. So it keeps working for the biggest earthquakes. Option A describes intensity, not magnitude. Option B is not the reason. Option D is wrong, because the Richter scale is logarithmic.

    Concept Tested: Moment Magnitude Scale

    See: Magnitude Scales

    7. Your station records a P wave, and the S wave arrives 5 seconds later. About how far away was the earthquake?¶

    1. About 5 km
    2. About 30 km
    3. About 40 km
    4. About 17 km
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. A seismic wave carries an earthquake's energy through rock. P waves travel faster than S waves, so the gap between them grows with distance, about 8 km for every second. Here, 5 × 8 = 40 km. Option A treats each second as one kilometre. Option B multiplies by the P wave speed alone. Option D multiplies by the S wave speed alone. Three stations doing this can locate the epicenter.

    Concept Tested: Seismic Wave

    See: Seismic Waves

    8. How many times greater is the ground motion amplitude of a magnitude 7.0 earthquake than a magnitude 4.0 earthquake?¶

    1. 3 times
    2. 30 times
    3. About 32,000 times
    4. 1,000 times
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The Richter scale is logarithmic. Each whole step means 10 times the ground motion amplitude. Three steps means 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 times. Option A treats the scale like an ordinary number line. Option B multiplies the steps by 10 instead of multiplying by 10 three times. Option C is the energy ratio, not the ground motion ratio, because each step releases about 32 times more energy.

    Concept Tested: Richter Scale

    See: Magnitude Scales

    9. You are setting up a MEMS accelerometer to detect earthquakes. Which settings fit the job best?¶

    1. A ±2 g range and 100 samples per second
    2. A ±16 g range and 100 samples per second
    3. A ±2 g range and 10 samples per second
    4. A ±16 g range and 10 samples per second
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. An accelerometer measures acceleration, and a MEMS accelerometer does it with a tiny mass on silicon springs. To catch a P wave and separate it from an S wave, you need at least 50 samples per second, and 100 is better. A ±2 g range gives eight times the resolution of ±16 g. Options B and D waste resolution on a huge range. Options C and D sample too slowly.

    Concept Tested: Accelerometer and MEMS Accelerometer

    See: Small, Cheap, and Everywhere

    10. During an earthquake, a city 100 km from the epicenter gets about 20 seconds of warning. A town 10 km away gets none. What best explains the difference?¶

    1. The town's sensors were broken, so no warning could be sent
    2. Radio signals travel more slowly near the epicenter of an earthquake
    3. Scientists predicted the earthquake for the city but not for the town
    4. Near the epicenter, the shaking arrives before a warning can be processed and sent
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Earthquake early warning detects the fast P wave and sends an alert by radio or internet, which travels far faster than seismic waves. Far from the epicenter, the alert outruns the shaking. Close in, there is a blind zone where shaking arrives before any warning can go out. Option A is not needed to explain it. Option B is false. Option C is wrong, because early warning is not prediction.

    Concept Tested: Earthquake Early Warning

    See: Earthquake Early Warning
