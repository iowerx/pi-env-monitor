# References: Barometric Pressure: The Weight of the Atmosphere

1. [Atmospheric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure) - Wikipedia - Explains air pressure as the weight of the air above, how it falls with altitude, and how sea level pressure is reported. Its tables of standard pressure in different units support the chapter's unit section.

2. [Barometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barometer) - Wikipedia - Traces the barometer from Torricelli's mercury tube to the aneroid and modern electronic sensors. Diagrams of each design and notes on weather forecasting match the instrument story told in this chapter.

3. [Piezoresistive effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezoresistive_effect) - Wikipedia - Describes how the electrical resistance of silicon changes when it is squeezed or stretched. It explains the mechanism inside the station's pressure sensor, where a tiny silicon membrane bends with changing air pressure.

4. Conceptual Physics - Paul G. Hewitt - Pearson - Hewitt describes us as living at the bottom of an "ocean of air" and compares equal-weight columns of air, mercury, and water. This picture-first approach makes the weight of the atmosphere and the barometer intuitive without heavy math.

5. The History of the Barometer - W. E. Knowles Middleton - Johns Hopkins Press - Middleton, a meteorologist and historian, rebuilt the barometer's story from original letters and instruments, from Torricelli and Pascal to the aneroid. Its many illustrations of historic barometers remain the standard reference on the subject.

6. [Torricellian Experiment](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/indepth/TorricellianExperiment.html) - Museo Galileo - The Florence science museum describes exactly how Torricelli filled and inverted his mercury tube in 1644. It also explains the heated debate over the vacuum, matching the chapter's story of Aristotle versus Torricelli.

7. [Air Pressure](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/air-pressure) - NOAA JetStream - Explains air pressure, how it decreases with height, and how pascals, millibars, hectopascals, and inches of mercury compare. Its standard sea level values help students check their own unit conversions.

8. [Under Pressure](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/under-pressure) - PhET Interactive Simulations - A simulation where students measure pressure at different depths in a fluid, with or without an atmosphere. It makes hydrostatic pressure visible and shows why pressure grows with the weight of fluid overhead.

9. [Earth Atmosphere Model](https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/atmos.html) - NASA Glenn Research Center - Shows the equations NASA uses to estimate air pressure and temperature at different altitudes. Its graphs help students see why pressure drops with height and how pressure altitude is calculated.

10. [Chapter 8: Flight Instruments](https://www.faa.gov/regulationspolicies/handbooksmanuals/aviation/phak/chapter-8-flight-instruments) - Federal Aviation Administration - A free chapter from the official pilot's handbook explaining the aneroid altimeter and how pilots set it to local pressure. Its diagrams show why a wrong pressure setting is dangerous for flight.
