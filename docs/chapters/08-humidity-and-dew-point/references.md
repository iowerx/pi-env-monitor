# References: Humidity and Dew Point: The Water Hidden in the Air

1. [Humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity) - Wikipedia - Explains absolute humidity, relative humidity, and specific humidity, with the role of vapor pressure and temperature. Its graphs show why warm air can hold far more water vapor than cold air.

2. [Dew point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dew_point) - Wikipedia - Defines the dew point as the temperature where air becomes saturated and water condenses. Its tables linking dew point to comfort, and its formulas, help students calculate dew point from their station's readings.

3. [Hygrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hygrometer) - Wikipedia - Describes the full family of humidity instruments: hair hygrometers, psychrometers, chilled mirrors, and capacitive sensors. It follows the same instrument lineage as the chapter, ending with the thin-film sensor in the BME280.

4. The Invention of Clouds - Richard Hamblyn - Farrar, Straus and Giroux - Hamblyn tells how amateur scientist Luke Howard gave clouds the Latin names cumulus, stratus, and cirrus in 1802. The book shows how naming and classifying clouds turned vague observations into shared science.

5. Invention of the Meteorological Instruments - W. E. Knowles Middleton - Johns Hopkins Press - Middleton traces each weather instrument from its first crude version onward, including hair hygrometers and the wet-and-dry-bulb psychrometer. His careful drawings and descriptions show how humidity measurement slowly became reliable.

6. [Dew Point vs Humidity](https://www.weather.gov/arx/why_dewpoint_vs_humidity) - National Weather Service - A short explainer on why forecasters prefer dew point over relative humidity to describe how muggy it feels. Its worked comparison of a cold and a warm day clears up a common misunderstanding.

7. [Water Cycle Diagrams](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-cycle-diagrams) - U.S. Geological Survey - Free, downloadable water cycle diagrams at several reading levels and in dozens of languages. They show evaporation, condensation, and precipitation, giving students a picture of where the water in the air comes from.

8. [Clouds and How They Form](https://scied.ucar.edu/learning-zone/clouds/how-clouds-form) - UCAR Center for Science Education - Explains how rising, cooling air reaches its dew point and forms clouds. Clear illustrations connect convection, condensation, and cloud formation, all key ideas from this chapter.

9. [What Are Wet Bulb Temperatures?](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2025/what-wet-bulb-temperatures-are-and-what-they-are-used-for) - Met Office - Explains how a thermometer wrapped in a wet wick cools by evaporation and how this measures humidity. It supports the chapter's section on the psychrometer and wet bulb temperature.

10. [Water Vapor](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/global-maps/water-vapor/) - NASA Earth Observatory - Monthly satellite maps showing how much water vapor is in the atmosphere around the world. Students can see seasonal changes and connect warm regions with moist air.
