# Quiz: Wind: Measuring Air in Motion

Test your understanding of pressure systems, fronts, the pressure gradient, wind direction and speed instruments, the Beaufort scale, wind units, wind chill, and wind load with these review questions.

!!! mascot-tip "From, Not Toward"
    ![Mecha pointing out a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Wind direction always names where the wind comes from. Say it to yourself before any direction question, and you will dodge the trap that catches almost everyone once.

---

#### 1. A weather vane's pointer swings around and aims due west. What wind direction should your station log?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 90°
2. 270°
3. 180°
4. 0°
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A weather vane pivots so its large tail is pushed downwind and its pointer aims into the wind. A pointer aimed west means the wind is coming from the west. Wind direction is the direction the wind comes from, in degrees clockwise from north, so west is 270°. Option A, 90°, is east, which is where the wind is going. Option C is south, and option D is north.

    **Concept Tested:** Wind Direction and Weather Vane

    **See:** [Direction and the Vane](index.md#direction-and-the-vane)

#### 2. What advantage does a sonic anemometer have over a cup anemometer?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It costs much less and is easier to build at home
2. It is never affected by ice or heavy rain
3. It must be aimed into the wind to give a reading
4. It has no moving parts, so it has no stall speed or inertia
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. A sonic anemometer times ultrasonic pulses between fixed transducers, so nothing spins. A cup anemometer has spinning cups that stall below about 0.5 m/s and are slow to spin up and slow down. Option A is backward, because sonic anemometers are expensive. Option B is wrong, because ice and rain disrupt the transducers. Option C is wrong, because neither instrument needs aiming. That was a problem with pressure plates.

    **Concept Tested:** Sonic Anemometer and Cup Anemometer

    **See:** [Sonic Anemometers](index.md#sonic-anemometers)

#### 3. What does the Enhanced Fujita scale use to rate a tornado?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The damage it causes, which is used to infer its wind speed
2. The wind speed measured by anemometers inside the tornado
3. The lowest air pressure recorded at the tornado's center
4. The width and length of the tornado's path on the ground
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Enhanced Fujita scale rates tornadoes from EF0 to EF5 based on damage. Engineers survey the damage afterward and infer the wind speed needed to cause it. Option B is not possible in practice, because tornadoes destroy anemometers. Options C and D are not what the scale uses. This is measurement by consequence rather than by instrument, which is unusual among scales.

    **Concept Tested:** Enhanced Fujita Scale

    **See:** [What Wind Does To Structures](index.md#what-wind-does-to-structures)

#### 4. Why does a low pressure system usually bring clouds and rain?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Air sinks, warms as it is squeezed, and moves away from saturation
2. Air rises, cools as it expands, and its moisture condenses
3. Air is pulled in from the ocean, and ocean air always carries rain
4. Low pressure lets more sunlight reach the ground, which builds storms
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A low pressure system forms where air is rising. Rising air expands and cools, moves toward saturation, and its moisture condenses into cloud and rain. Option A describes a high pressure system, where sinking air warms by compression and clouds dissolve. That is why highs bring fair, settled weather. Options C and D are not the mechanism the chapter describes.

    **Concept Tested:** Low Pressure System and High Pressure System

    **See:** [Highs, Lows, and Fronts](index.md#highs-lows-and-fronts)

#### 5. You are looking at a weather map with isobars but no wind data. Where should the strongest winds be?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Where the central pressure is lowest on the map
2. Where the central pressure is highest on the map
3. Where the isobars are spaced farthest apart
4. Where the isobars are packed closest together
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The pressure gradient is how fast pressure changes over distance. Isobars connect points of equal pressure, so closely packed isobars mean a steep gradient and strong wind. Option A is the common mistake. A very low pressure with gentle gradients around it gives only light winds. Option B makes the same error. Option C is backward, because widely spaced isobars mean a gentle gradient and light wind.

    **Concept Tested:** Pressure Gradient

    **See:** [The Pressure Gradient](index.md#the-pressure-gradient)

#### 6. Why does a cold wind make you feel colder than the thermometer reads?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Wind lowers the actual air temperature around you
2. Wind carries moisture that soaks into your clothes and skin
3. Wind strips away the thin layer of warm air next to your skin
4. Wind cools your body to a temperature below the air temperature
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Wind chill is apparent temperature in the cold, meaning how cold it feels. Your body warms a thin film of air against your skin, and that film insulates you. Wind strips it away, so you lose heat faster. Option A is wrong, because wind does not change the air temperature. Option D is a common misconception, because wind cannot cool anything below the air temperature. Option B is not the mechanism.

    **Concept Tested:** Wind Chill and Apparent Temperature

    **See:** [What Wind Does To People](index.md#what-wind-does-to-people)

#### 7. Your station has no anemometer yet. You see small trees swaying in the wind. What should you log?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Beaufort force 5, about 8 to 11 m/s
2. Beaufort force 2, about 2 to 3 m/s
3. Beaufort force 8, about 17 to 21 m/s
4. Beaufort force 10, about 25 to 28 m/s
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The Beaufort scale relates wind speed to effects you can see. Small trees swaying matches force 5, a fresh breeze of about 8.0 to 10.7 m/s. Force 2 (B) is rustling leaves. Force 8 (C) breaks twigs off trees. Force 10 (D) uproots trees. Remember that the Beaufort scale is ordinal, so force 8 is not twice force 4. Log a note that this reading came from your eyes, not an instrument.

    **Concept Tested:** Beaufort Scale

    **See:** [The Beaufort Scale](index.md#the-beaufort-scale)

#### 8. Your station stores a wind speed of 15 m/s. A pilot asks for it in knots. About what should you report?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. About 29 knots
2. About 54 knots
3. About 34 knots
4. About 8 knots
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Meters per second is the SI unit your station stores. A knot is one nautical mile per hour, used in aviation and marine work. To convert, multiply by 1.944: 15 × 1.944 ≈ 29 knots. The quick rule, "roughly double it," gives 30. Option B multiplies by 3.6, which gives km/h. Option C multiplies by 2.237, which gives mph. Option D divides instead of multiplying.

    **Concept Tested:** Knot Unit and Meters Per Second

    **See:** [Units](index.md#units)

#### 9. During a storm, the wind rises from 10 m/s to 30 m/s. How many times greater is the force on a wall?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 3 times
2. 6 times
3. 9 times
4. 27 times
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Wind load is the force wind puts on a structure, and it grows with the square of wind speed. The speed tripled, so the force grows by 3² = 9 times. Option A assumes force grows evenly with speed. Option B doubles the tripling. Option D cubes the change instead of squaring it. This is why a storm that is only somewhat faster can be far more destructive.

    **Concept Tested:** Wind Load

    **See:** [What Wind Does To Structures](index.md#what-wind-does-to-structures)

#### 10. Your station records this within one hour: pressure falls and then rises sharply, wind speed spikes and the direction shifts suddenly, and temperature and dew point both drop several degrees. What most likely passed?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A warm front
2. A high pressure system settling in
3. A cold front
4. A tornado
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. A weather front is the boundary between two air masses. At a cold front, dense cold air wedges under warm air and forces it up sharply. The result is a quick, dramatic change: a pressure dip and sharp rise, a wind spike and shift, and falling temperature and dew point. A warm front (A) passes slowly over many hours. A high (B) brings calm, settled weather. Nothing in the data points to a tornado (D).

    **Concept Tested:** Weather Front

    **See:** [Highs, Lows, and Fronts](index.md#highs-lows-and-fronts)
