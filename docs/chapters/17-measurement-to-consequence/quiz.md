# Quiz: From Measurement to Consequence

Test your understanding of how measurements drive forecasts, warnings, farming, aviation, building codes, heat islands, wildfire risk, the climate record, and data sharing with these review questions.

    For each question, trace the chain from instrument to record to decision. Ask which measurement feeds the decision, and the right answer usually comes into focus.

#### 1. What is evapotranspiration?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Water lost by evaporation from soil plus transpiration from plants
2. Rainwater soaking down into the soil after a storm
3. Water vapor condensing on grass as dew overnight
4. Water running off pavement and into storm drains
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Evapotranspiration is the combined loss of water from a surface by evaporation from soil and transpiration from plants. It tells a farmer how much water a field really needs. Working it out takes four of your station's measurements: temperature, humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation. Option B describes water soaking in, not leaving. Option C is condensation. Option D is runoff.

    **Concept Tested:** Evapotranspiration

    **See:** [Temperature Decides When Things Grow and What They Cost](index.md#temperature-decides-when-things-grow-and-what-they-cost)

#### 2. Which statement about weather forecasts and severe weather warnings is correct?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Forecast models can run from physics alone, with no fresh observations
2. A warning means conditions are only favorable, so you should just prepare
3. Models start from a snapshot built from observations, and a warning means act now
4. A watch is more urgent than a warning and means you should act at once
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Weather forecasting uses models that need an accurate picture of the atmosphere right now, built from thousands of readings. Every reading improves that starting point. A severe weather warning means danger is happening or about to happen, so act now. Option A is wrong, because models cannot start from nothing. Option B describes a watch. Option D reverses watch and warning.

    **Concept Tested:** Weather Forecasting and Severe Weather Warning

    **See:** [Pressure Predicts, and Keeps Aircraft Apart](index.md#pressure-predicts-and-keeps-aircraft-apart)

#### 3. Why is the temperature gap between a city and the nearby countryside usually biggest after dark?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Cities receive more sunshine in the evening than rural areas do
2. Concrete and brick store heat all day and release it at night
3. Grass gives off stored heat at night, which cools the countryside
4. Air conditioners switch off at night and release their trapped heat
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. An urban heat island is a built-up area that is much warmer than the countryside around it. Concrete and brick have high thermal mass. They soak up heat all day and release it all night, so the gap peaks after dark and can top 10 °C on clear, calm nights. Option A is false. Option C is backward, because grass does not store heat like concrete. Option D is not why the gap peaks at night.

    **Concept Tested:** Urban Heat Island

    **See:** [Cities Make Their Own Weather](index.md#cities-make-their-own-weather)

#### 4. A maize farmer uses a base temperature of 10 °C. Today's high was 26 °C, and the low was 14 °C. How many growing degree days did today add?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 10
2. 20
3. 30
4. 16
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Agricultural planning often tracks growing degree days, because crops develop by built-up warmth, not by calendar days. The formula is the average of the high and low, minus the base: (26 + 14) ÷ 2 − 10 = 20 − 10 = 10. Option B forgets to subtract the base. Option C adds the high and low but forgets to divide by 2. Option D subtracts the base from the high only.

    **Concept Tested:** Agricultural Planning

    **See:** [Temperature Decides When Things Grow and What They Cost](index.md#temperature-decides-when-things-grow-and-what-they-cost)

#### 5. Which afternoon has the highest wildfire risk?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 30 °C, 70% humidity, calm air, and rain yesterday
2. 18 °C, 20% humidity, a light breeze, and rain two days ago
3. 33 °C, 10% humidity, strong wind, and no rain for 40 days
4. 35 °C, 85% humidity, strong wind, and heavy rain this morning
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Wildfire risk combines low relative humidity, high temperature, wind speed, and days since rain. Option C is hot, very dry, and windy after a long dry spell, which is the most dangerous combination. Option A has humid, calm air and recent rain. Option B is dry but cool, with little wind and recent rain. Option D is hot and windy, but humid and freshly soaked, so the fuel is wet.

    **Concept Tested:** Wildfire Risk

    **See:** [Cities Make Their Own Weather](index.md#cities-make-their-own-weather)

#### 6. An airliner climbs above the transition altitude. What pressure do all aircraft set on their altimeters, and why?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Their departure airport's pressure, so each one knows its exact height
2. A standard 1013.25 hPa, so every altimeter is off by the same amount
3. Zero hectopascals, so the altimeters read true distance above the ground
4. The pressure at their own altitude, updated every minute
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Aviation safety depends on pressure, because an altimeter is a barometer. Above the transition altitude, every aircraft sets a standard 1013.25 hPa. Their altimeters may all be slightly wrong, but they are wrong in exactly the same way, so planes stay safely apart. Option A would leave aircraft from different airports using different settings. Options C and D are not how altimeters are set.

    **Concept Tested:** Aviation Safety

    **See:** [Pressure Predicts, and Keeps Aircraft Apart](index.md#pressure-predicts-and-keeps-aircraft-apart)

#### 7. Which pairing correctly matches each warning system to the idea that makes it work?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Tsunami warning needs dense rain gauges; flash flood warning uses seismic wave speed
2. Both warnings depend only on river level gauges placed along the coast
3. Tsunami warning predicts earthquakes days ahead; flash flood warning needs no sensors
4. Tsunami warning uses the speed gap with slow ocean waves; flash flood warning needs dense rain gauges
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Tsunami warning works because seismic waves travel 6 km/s and alerts travel at light speed, while a tsunami crosses the ocean at about 700 km/h. Distant coasts get hours of notice. Flood warning for flash floods needs dense rainfall measurement, because an intense storm over a small area can slip past a sparse network. Option A swaps the two ideas. Option B ignores seismic detection. Option C is wrong, because earthquakes cannot be predicted.

    **Concept Tested:** Tsunami Warning and Flood Warning

    **See:** [Wind and Ground Motion Are Written Into Buildings](index.md#wind-and-ground-motion-are-written-into-buildings)

#### 8. A town's temperature record jumps 1.5 °C in 1975, the year its station moved from a field to a spot beside an airport runway. What should climatologists do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Find and document the move, then adjust for it in a separate correction
2. Delete every reading taken before 1975, because they are now wrong
3. Leave the record as it is, because any correction is manipulation
4. Treat the jump as proof of a sudden change in the climate in 1975
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A climate record is a long series of measurements, often built from many instruments and locations. Station moves create jumps that must be found, documented, and adjusted, with the original readings left untouched. Option B destroys real data. Option C is a misconception. Without corrections, a record from changing sites means nothing. Option D mistakes a station move for climate. The timing points straight to the move.

    **Concept Tested:** Climate Record

    **See:** [The Long Record](index.md#the-long-record)

#### 9. Which opening sentence for your project report best follows the chapter's advice on science communication?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. "We wired a BME280 to a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W with four jumper wires."
2. "Our data proves that blacktop everywhere is dangerous to everyone."
3. "Temperature readings were taken every 60 seconds for 14 weeks."
4. "On calm nights, the blacktop stayed 4 °C warmer than the garden, the gap between a mild night and a hot one."
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Science communication means explaining your work to people who were not there. The chapter says to lead with the finding, not the method, and to give the number a comparison so it means something. Option D does both. Options A and C open with method details that belong later in the report. Option B claims far more than one school station can show, and it ignores the limitations you should state.

    **Concept Tested:** Science Communication

    **See:** [What To Do With Yours](index.md#what-to-do-with-yours)

#### 10. You are preparing a year of station data to share with a citizen science network. Which package would you create?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A cleaned CSV with every outlier deleted, so users see only good data
2. A single chart image showing the whole year of temperature
3. A CSV with short names like temp and press, plus your email for questions
4. A CSV with units in every column and quality flags kept, plus metadata, a licence, and a stable home
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Citizen science networks feed volunteer data into real scientific products, so data sharing has to be done well. Shared data needs a readable format, units in every column, quality flags kept, a full metadata file, a licence, and a stable location. Option A hides problems, which makes data less trustworthy. Option B cannot be analyzed. Option C leaves out units and metadata, so users must guess.

    **Concept Tested:** Data Sharing and Citizen Science

    **See:** [What To Do With Yours](index.md#what-to-do-with-yours)
